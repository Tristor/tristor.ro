It's been about two years since my last post, a lot has happened in my life but that's probably for another time.  I decided I'd start writing again with a trip report as I just returned from my very first DEFCON, DEFCON 34 which was August 6th to August 9th, 2026 in Las Vegas at the LVCC.  

### August 5th: Travel Day

Not much to say, was uneventful. I traveled with a long-time friend who went to his first DEFCON last year and encouraged me to finally go. We got into Vegas on Wednesday the 5th fairly late, got checked into our hotel, had dinner, and called it a night.

### August 6th: Linecon

Registration opened at 8AM, and we were in line by 7:30AM and it was still just under an hour to get to the front of the line and get our badges.  From registration we headed over the merch line, which we entered at around 8:24AM and got to the merch table over 3 hours later around 11:50AM.  After acquiring our merch, we wandered the LVCC to identify where everything was going to be at.  The first day is primarily registration and merch, and everything really opens the following day.  This year was an electronic badge year, and so we used the rest of the day to commence with badge hacking.

[Famed hardware hacker Andrew "bunnie" Huang worked with a team to design and produce the DC34 badges](https://www.wired.com/story/defcon-34-badge-baochip-andrew-bunnie-huang/) and is one of the first shipping devices with the Baochip 1X which has four RISC-V cores, 6MB of memory, built-in security functions, and a fully open source BIOS and OS written in Rust.  The badge additional included a camera module and a printed circuit board with a peacock design as well as connections to power two SAOs (badge add-ons).  The DC34 badge is designed to easily convert to act as a hardware security module that supports FIDO2 similar to a Yubikey after the conference, and all of the code and documentation is in the open on [the DEFCON 34 Badge page](https://defcon.org/34b/).

I reached out on the Meshtastic mesh to see if anyone wanted to join me in the food court area of Resorts World, and I got some replies, but ultimately nobody came to visit.  My friend and I jammed for around 3 hours or so on the badge and in that time frame I manage to brick/reset mine, update to a newer firmware release, get the official dc34-image tool working to upload a custom badge logo image, immediately break that, start over again and then put a custom badge logo image on it, and figure out how light genes and the light combinations work for the RGB LEDs built into the badge.  There was a very active Discord for folks working on badge hacking and by the end of the conference [other folks](https://www.anthonymattas.com/articles/only-132-bytes) [had figured out the shared secret key](https://github.com/nastea1/dc34badge) for the light gene generation and [made some](https://github.com/nastea1/dc34-gamete) [tools](https://ph1r3574r73r.github.io/DEFCON34_Badge/) that allow you to generate specific light genes at will.  The team at Cheeso that helped design the badge later published a [contest for folks placing the peacock PCB](https://bird.cheeso.io/#log).

While I didn't focus as much attention on the badge as I did on other things during the conference and it went beyond my depth as a hardware hacker, I really enjoyed the experience of poking at it over a serial interface and learning by watching other's progress in Discord and Github.  Now that the conference is over, I'll probably use the badge as a research tool to learn more about RISC-V, the Rust code of the open OS is also really useful for reading and understanding how to work with RISC-V directly.

### August 7th: 5n4ck3y + Locktopus Qualifiers

The friend who was with me had spent a lot of time on a particular CTF called [5n4ck3y](https://x.com/5n4ck3y?lang=en) which is a converted vending machine that provides an approachable multi-disciplinary CTF that combines puzzles, games, hardware hacking, binary cracking, debugging, radio/RF, and other aspects.  Participants progressively gain flags through various means which require interactions with the physical vending machine until you have achieved enough flags to receive a badge from the vending machine (while supplies last), which then begins the second half of the CTF which is primarily focused on hardware hacking the badge via a serial interface.  Last year my friend spent a lot of time on this CTF but was unable to get a badge, this year we worked together and rapidly got half the needed flags before teaming up with a group of folks at the tables near the CTF to get flags which required equipment we didn't bring like a Flipper Zero (for infrared) and an RTL-SDR (for the radio/RF pieces).  We were able to ultimately work with them so that our entire group received the badge.

We finished all the necessary flags for the 5n4ck3y badge near the end of that day for the villages, but I came primarily to DEFCON for lockpicking and had to rush over to be in the last round of Locktopus qualifies in the Lockpicking Village, operated by [TOOOL](https://toool.us/).  [Locktopus](https://github.com/locktopus-project/locktopus) is an open source software/hardware combination that's used to create locksport games.  The basic premise is that 8 competitors surround the device, which uses electronic sensors and a timer to track how long it takes you to get an open for a specially constructed padlock body with different cores.  For this year's challenge, there were four locks marked A, B, C, and D and competitors rotated around the table until they'd attempted each lock.  You received a maximum time of 5 minutes per round, with a default score of 5 minutes, and the fastest total time was the winner.  The contest was constructed in three brackets: Qualifiers, Semi-Finals, and Finals with the 32 fastest times in Qualifiers advancing to the Semi-Finals and being split into 4 groups of 8.  The two fastest folks at each of the groups in Semi-Finals advanced to the Finals (top 8), and in Finals the winners and rankings were determined by total time.

I went into the competition not thinking I'd make it very far, honestly not even expecting to get through Qualifiers, but I wanted to give a go.  I've been active in local lockpicking/locksport groups for about 8 months, and while I don't have my LPU belt (because I don't want to record videos of myself), I have roughly a blue belt equivalent skill level currently based on the locks I've successfully picked in front of witnesses at meets.  During qualifiers I realized quickly that the locks could be raked and set about doing this as rapidly as possible.  I started on Lock B, and I had begun single-pin picking and then switched to raking with a 4 minute, 35 second open time.  On Lock C I started with raking and opened in 19.74 seconds, on Lock D I raked in 10.79 seconds, and on Lock A raked in 8.53 seconds for a total time of 5 minutes and 14 seconds.  This was fast enough to make it into Semi-Finals the following day.


With that it was around 6PM that day and the villages closed.  There was a set of parties later in the day but before then I went to dinner with my friend and then we went back over to go to a few of the parties.  Then off to bed.


### August 8th: 5n4ck3y Badge + Locktopus Semi-Finals and Finals + Crack The Core (CTC)

I got in line early so I could get to the line for 5n4ck3y first thing when the villages opened, the lines had been long throughout the event the prior day.  I already had a vend code for my badge from the previous day but had been unable to get back over after Locktopus qualifiers and didn't want to miss out on the badge and exploring it.  My friend stayed in the line and got his badge the previous day as did the rest of our group.  I bee-lined it, yet the line was still very long by the time I got there and I waited roughly an hour and 20 minutes, but was able to vend my badge.  While in line near the end I was approached by one of the teams in the top 3 to see if I would be willing to let them dump my badge or if I planned to continue with the competition.  I had decided since I made it into Semi-Finals for Locktopus I would focus there, and there was an additional locksport CTF called [Crack The Core](https://crackthecore.com/) that I wanted to participate in.  I let them dump my badge and they mentioned I would be credited in their later write-up (I don't believe its been published as of my writing this post).  That team was second place and I believe won the CTF.  They were nice and explained a lot of stuff quickly about doing serial debugging and how to interact with the badge while dumping it, which came in handy later so I could do some basic experimentation myself.

I headed over after getting my 5n4ck3y badge to the vendors and did some shopping, acquiring some goodies from Hak5, iFixIt, and Electronic Cats, donating to the EFF and Tor Project, and chatting with the folks at Low Mesh and Rokland.  After I finished up there, I went back to the lockpicking village and competed in the Semi-Finals.  In my Semi-Finals round nobody was able to successfully pick Lock B, Lock C, or Lock D within the allotted time including myself, so the total time was determined by speed on Lock A.  I raked Lock A in 11.19 seconds, which was the fastest time at the table and advanced me to the Finals nearly immediately after (around 3:45PM).  In the Finals I started raking Lock D but it seemed like the pins were oversetting with raking, so I switched to a McTickler and zipped it (miraculously on my first try) for an open time of 32.52 seconds.  Moving to Lock A, which had not been opened by the previous folks, and I also was unable to open it.  Moving to lock B, I raked it in 11.28 seconds, and observed that all the competitors had successfully raked Lock C which would be my final lock.  Moving to Lock C, I began raking but was unable to get an open.  Around a minute and 40 seconds into raking, I was using a more aggressive raking technique and slipped stabbing myself in the hand and bleeding fairly profusely, however I continued by switching to single-pin picking.  Unfortunately I was not able to open Lock C in the allotted time.  My total time in Finals was 10 minutes 43.8 seconds, which put me at 5th place overall. For those of my readers not as familiar with lockpicking, 5 minutes really isn't much time to pick a lock using single-pin picking, unless you have a golden touch which I very much do not.  I was pleasantly surprised to make it into Finals and I am very happy with a 5th place overall finish.

After finishing the Finals around 4PM-ish, I went over to find my friend who had spent the day working on Crack the Core.  I signed up a bit late but got to work.  Crack the Core is a progressive lockpicking CTF, where picking the lock reveals a flag that you can submit for points, harder locks gain more points, and the leaderboard is based on cumulative points. Competitors must utilize their own tools and compete as individuals without sharing flags, unlike most CTFs which allow teamwork.  I zipped through the locks on the beginners table, except for the very first one which I struggled with (and apparently the person who at that time was #4 on the leaderboard had also struggled with it, they shared with me) and started picking more difficult locks to generate points.  We finished the day with my friend at #9 on the leaderboard and I was somewhere around #30.  

We went and had a very nice celebratory dinner at Kusa Nori, where I indulged in some A5 Wagyu and my favorite Japanese whisky, and then my friend went to the much more lively parties on Saturday night while I went back to my room to work on the 5n4ck3y badge and set up and learn about some of the new tools and equipment I had acquired.  I was ultimately able to get some assistance on a tool I was missing for the 5n4ck3y badge from another person via the mesh (thanks DEFCON meshers for being awesome) and submitted a few more flags.

### August 9th: Crack the Core (CTC) and Wrapping Up

My friend and I both headed directly to Crack The Core and started working on locks until Noon when the villages closed and all the CTFs officially ended.  My final ranking was 25th overall in Crack The Core and his was 17th overall.  I went to a great talk on travel security, and then went with my friend back over to the vendors so he could pick some things up.  We left a bit early to just chill and decompress and then met up for dinner before getting ready for the closing party that was at 10:30PM at LIV nightclub inside the Fontainebleau.  I checked our rankings on 5n4ck3y and I ended up 115th overall, which is pretty good for not doing much at all on the second half.

### August 10th: Return Travel

During our trip back home we connected through the Denver airport and they had 4 TSA agents at the gate checking everyone's IDs as they boarded.  I was absolutely livid to see this happening, as I had never encountered this in many years of extensive traveling.  I'm a United 1K and both myself and my friend got upgraded as we were traveling on a group booking, I was the first to board and questioned why they were IDing people when the airline already knows who we are and the airline is typically responsible for ID checks for things like passports on international flights.  They just said it was a "random ID check" and wouldn't provide any further information.  I challenged the legality of it and told them I didn't want to provide ID and tried to hand my boarding pass to the gate agent, and they called over 2 additional TSA agents (6 total) to surround me and threatened me with denied boarding and potential arrest if I didn't provide ID.  I ultimately showed my Global Entry card and boarded the flight, deciding it wasn't worth not getting back home to my wife and inconveniencing my friend (or potentially getting him into trouble).  Suffice to say, I *DO NOT* like this type of Stasi "papers, please" bullshit, and I think the US is rolling off a cliff at high speed currently.

Thanks to our flight already being delayed and the extra delay from the TSA, we ended up not getting home until nearly 2AM on the 11th.

That's pretty much a wrap.
